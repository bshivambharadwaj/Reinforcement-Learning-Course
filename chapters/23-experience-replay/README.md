## 23.1 Turn experience into a reusable dataset

Experience replay stores past transitions and samples them for later updates. It improves data reuse and changes the temporal dependence seen by the optimizer. It also makes the training distribution a design choice.

**Prerequisites:** Chapters 20–22. **Targets:** specify buffer semantics, quantify data reuse, and reason about stale and prioritized samples.

A courier's archive can teach from rare failures long after they occur. An archive dominated by obsolete routes, however, can train an inaccurate picture of today's traffic.

<a id="section-23-2"></a>

## 23.2 Define the stored transition contract

Store observation, executed action, reward, final next observation, termination, and truncation separately when needed. Include action masks or the information required to reconstruct them. For recurrent models, a single isolated transition may not supply the hidden-state context.

Do not store a mutable reference that the environment later overwrites. A replay buffer whose old observations silently change is no longer a dataset of the collected trajectories.

<a id="section-23-3"></a>

## 23.3 Calculate the reuse budget

Suppose a buffer is full with C entries, each environment step inserts one transition, and U updates of batch size B occur per step. Under uniform sampling, a particular stored item appears in expectation UB/C times per step.

It remains for about C steps in a FIFO ring buffer, giving approximately UB total sampled appearances before eviction. With U=2 and B=32, this is 64 appearances. Capacity affects age and diversity even when this steady-state expected reuse stays the same.

### Calculate the age distribution in a full FIFO buffer

With one new transition per environment step and uniform sampling from a full capacity-C FIFO buffer, stored ages run approximately from 0 to C−1. Their mean is (C−1)/2 steps. Capacity therefore controls how far training looks into past behavior even when the expected total reuse per transition stays near UB.

For C=1,000 the mean age is 499.5 steps; for C=100,000 it is 49,999.5. If the environment or policy changes quickly, those samples can reflect different conditions. Log age in environment steps rather than assuming buffer size directly equals elapsed wall time.

<a id="section-23-4"></a>

## 23.4 Distinguish decorrelation from independence

Random sampling breaks the original sequential ordering within batches. It does not make the underlying transitions statistically independent: many may come from the same trajectory or nearby states, and all were generated by related policies.

For uncertainty estimates, count independent runs or appropriately grouped episodes rather than treating replay draws as fresh environmental evidence. Sampling the same transition 100 times improves optimization access, not the amount of observed world information.

<a id="section-23-5"></a>

## 23.5 Understand staleness and coverage

A larger buffer preserves diverse experiences and rare events, but may contain actions from old policies or a changed environment. A smaller buffer adapts quickly but can forget important outcomes and become highly correlated.

There is no capacity that is universally best. Measure sample age, task distribution, and how often critical transitions appear. If an environment changes, decide whether old transitions still correspond to the current model rather than assuming more data must help.

<a id="section-23-6"></a>

## 23.6 Derive prioritized replay weights

One scheme samples item i with P(i)=p_i^alpha/sum_j p_j^alpha, where p_i might be |TD error|+epsilon. For a uniform-buffer objective, an exact importance multiplier is 1/(N P(i)). Practical methods often use [1/(N P(i))]^beta, with beta<1 giving partial correction.

The identity E_(i~P)[f_i/(N P(i))]=(1/N)sum_i f_i requires positive support. Priorities must be updated as predictions change. High error can also indicate irreducible noise, so priority is not synonymous with usefulness.

### Derive the objective induced by partial correction

If item i is sampled with P(i) and weighted by [1/(N P(i))]^beta, its expected gradient coefficient is N^(−beta)P(i)^(1−beta). At beta=1 this becomes 1/N, recovering the uniform dataset objective under support assumptions. At beta=0 it is P(i), retaining full prioritization.

For intermediate beta, the expected update optimizes a reweighted objective when priorities and weights are treated as fixed for the update. If priorities depend on current parameters, practical methods usually do not differentiate through the sampling distribution. State that convention when deriving the intended gradient.

<a id="section-23-7"></a>

## 23.7 Work a prioritized example

With two items and sampling probabilities [0.8,0.2], uniform-objective weights are [0.625,2.5]. Their weighted contributions satisfy 0.8×0.625=0.5 and 0.2×2.5=0.5.

If weights are normalized by the maximum, they become [0.25,1]. That preserves their relative correction but scales the whole expected gradient. Without any correction, the training objective is intentionally reweighted toward frequently sampled items.

<a id="section-23-8"></a>

## 23.8 Know when arbitrary replay is inappropriate

Vanilla on-policy policy gradients assume data from the current policy. Reusing old trajectories without correction changes the expectation. PPO reuses a recent rollout for several epochs using old-policy ratios; this is not an unrestricted replay buffer.

For n-step targets, ensure stored sequences do not cross episode resets. For recurrent training, consider burn-in and hidden-state reconstruction. Buffer convenience should not override the mathematical data requirements of the learner.

### Preserve temporal context when replaying sequences

A recurrent policy or critic conditions on hidden state produced by earlier observations. Sampling an isolated transition and initializing memory to zero may provide a different input than the one used during collection. Storing the old hidden state also introduces staleness when network parameters change.

Sequence replay with a burn-in prefix can reconstruct a current hidden state before computing losses on later steps. Mask boundaries so burn-in never continues through an unrelated reset. Compare this with isolated-transition replay on a deliberately partially observed task, where remembering an earlier cue is necessary.

Use [Notebook 04](../../notebooks/04_dqn.ipynb) to vary capacity and update-to-data ratio separately. Record environment steps, gradient steps, and evaluation return. A prioritized-buffer implementation is a proposed extension, not a feature implied by the link.

## 23.10 Problems, worked answers, and reading

1. Under the steady-state approximation, how often is an item sampled with U=4 and B=16?
2. With N=4 and P(i)=0.1, what is the full importance weight?
3. Why is zero sampling probability for an item incompatible with recovering its uniform contribution?

<details><summary>Worked answers</summary>

1. Approximately UB=64 times before eviction, assuming uniform sampling and a full FIFO buffer.
2. 1/(4×0.1)=2.5.
3. No samples contain information about that item's contribution, and its inverse-probability weight is undefined. Positive support is essential to the identity.

</details>

Read [Prioritized Experience Replay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05952). Identify which parts correct a sampling distribution and which are heuristic prioritization choices.
